1. Final research direction Week 4

The project will move from broad citywide exploratory analysis to a five-ZIP screen and a focused comparison of 19132 and 19143. The five candidates show how market growth and resident vulnerability vary across the selection quadrants. The two upper-right ZIPs receive the detailed analysis of housing pressure, affordability, resident vulnerability, and possible displacement exposure.

The project should describe housing pressure and potential displacement risk. Gentrification is a broader neighborhood-change process involving market, socioeconomic, and demographic change. The current evidence does not establish that a ZIP has gentrified or that an individual household was displaced.

Week 5 will retain 19132 and 19143 as the detailed case studies while testing whether their selection is robust. The test will compare the two ZIPs with all qualifying Philadelphia ZIP/ZCTAs and the City as a whole, examine alternative property definitions, and add demographic-change evidence. The purpose is to contextualize the selected ZIPs, not restart the case-study selection without a documented reason.

Final research question Week 4

Do 19132 and 19143 continue to show unusual housing pressure and resident vulnerability when compared with all qualifying Philadelphia ZIP/ZCTAs and the City overall, and which responses best match the validated risks?

Analytical logic Week 4

Resident vulnerability and baseline housing conditions
    -> Housing-market change relative to Philadelphia
    -> Affordability and transaction pressure
    -> Demographic or housing-instability evidence
    -> Potential displacement risk
    -> Targeted City response

The final result should not be a collection of unrelated graphs. Every measure must help explain either the pressure occurring in a ZIP code, the residents who may be exposed, or the response that the City could consider.

2. Role of affordability, activity, and assessment measures Weeks 4-5

2.1 Price-to-income ratio: primary affordability indicator Weeks 4-5

Price-to-income ratio = median residential sale price
                        / median household income

This ratio evaluates whether local home prices are becoming increasingly disconnected from the incomes of households living in the ZIP code. A ratio of 4.0 means that the median sale price is approximately four times the median annual household income.

It helps answer:

  • Are prices increasing faster than local incomes?
  • Which detailed ZIP code has experienced greater affordability pressure?
  • Is that pressure persistent or driven by one unusual year?
  • How does each ZIP compare with Philadelphia overall?

This is a descriptive screening measure, not a complete homebuyer affordability calculation. It does not account for mortgage interest rates, down payments, property taxes, insurance, credit qualification, or the actual income of the households purchasing the homes.

2.2 Sale-to-assessment ratio: appendix diagnostic Weeks 4-5

Sale-to-assessment ratio = property sale price / assessed property value

This ratio evaluates how closely assessed values track observed sale prices. It is not a direct measure of homebuyer affordability.

  • A ratio near 1.0 means sale price and assessment are approximately aligned.
  • A ratio above 1.0 means the property sold for more than its assessed value.
  • A ratio below 1.0 means the property sold for less than its assessed value.

The ratio can support the displacement-risk theme indirectly. If market prices rise substantially above assessments, a later reassessment may increase property-tax exposure for existing owners. That exposure may be more important for lower-income, elderly, fixed-income, and longtime homeowners.

The final presentation should describe this measure as assessment alignment, not as proof of mortgage availability, political intent, tax-bill change, or buyer affordability. The main presentation should use transaction activity instead while assessment-year alignment remains unverified.

2.3 How the measures complement one another Week 5

Measure Main question Population most relevant
Price-to-income Are market prices aligned with local incomes? Prospective buyers and the wider community
Transaction activity Is recorded market activity increasing or decreasing? Neighborhood market; not a direct measure of household movement
Rent burden Can tenants continue to afford housing costs? Renters
Appendix sale-to-assessment How do recorded sale prices compare with the RTT assessed-value field? Existing property owners; interpretation limited by missing assessment year

The measures should remain separate. They should not be combined into one unexplained index.

Citywide medians across all qualifying Philadelphia candidates set the market-growth and vulnerability quadrant boundaries.   • 19138: higher growth and lower vulnerability;

Recommended selection matrix Week 4

Lower resident vulnerability Higher resident vulnerability
Lower market pressure Monitor and maintain baseline Prioritize resident support and stability
Higher market pressure Manage growth and monitor affordability Highest anti-displacement attention

Minimum selection criteria Week 4

  1. Adequate annual sale observations in both ZIP codes.
  2. Matched Redfin price and ACS income coverage through 2024.
  3. Acceptable ACS margins of error for the main indicators.
  4. A meaningful difference in either market pressure or vulnerability.
  5. No selection based solely on the most extreme observed value.

The team should record the rule and selected ZIP codes before interpreting the final results.

In Week 5, report where all qualifying Philadelphia ZIP/ZCTAs fall on the same measures, not only the five presentation ZIPs. The five-ZIP screen remains a readable slide; the full distribution belongs in the dashboard and validation tables. If the selected ZIPs change materially under defensible property-type, unit, or source definitions, document the sensitivity rather than hiding it.

The full Week 5 distribution shows that 19140 is the closest qualifying ZIP to 19132 on the growth/vulnerability quadrant: approximately 15.2% price CAGR and a 73.4% vulnerability percentile, compared with 17.0% and 73.9% for 19132. Therefore, 19132 and 19143 are a purposefully contrasting pair, not the two highest-scoring ZIPs. 19140 has adequate coverage and remains a close high-pressure benchmark; 19143 adds a higher current price, higher price-to-income ratio, greater renter exposure/burden, and a different transaction trajectory.

Required speaker note: Disclose the 19140 comparison on the selection slide. If the objective changes to selecting the two most extreme combined growth/vulnerability cases, rerun the downstream analysis using 19132 and 19140.

4. Analytical dimensions to build Weeks 4-5

4.1 Market pressure Weeks 4-5

Use Redfin and Philadelphia real-estate-transfer (RTT) data to calculate the same measures for 19132, 19143, every qualifying Philadelphia ZIP/ZCTA, and a clearly defined City benchmark:

  • nominal and inflation-adjusted median residential sale price;
  • five-year and ten-year price growth;
  • current price level relative to the Philadelphia median;
  • price growth relative to Philadelphia overall;
  • eligible sale counts and changes in transaction activity;
  • transaction counts and transactions per 1,000 occupied housing units;
  • price-growth speed, measured by CAGR, separately from acceleration, measured as the change in growth rate between pre-pandemic and recent periods;
  • the appendix sale-to-assessment ratio and its interquartile range; and
  • the stability of each trend after removing low-volume years.

The city comparison is necessary. A 30% increase in one ZIP is not necessarily an unusual local signal if Philadelphia overall also increased by 30%.

Property type and price-per-unit decision gate [Week 5]

The current Redfin ZIP series is aggregated and the current RTT extracts contain parcel counts, not residential-unit counts. property_count and n_parcel_rows must not be treated as the number of homes within a property. Complete the following checks in order:

  1. Compare Redfin All Residential trends with available Redfin property-type series, including single-family, condominium, and multifamily categories.
  2. Search for an OPA property-characteristics source containing residential-unit counts and a defensible parcel key.
  3. If the OPA join passes coverage and reconciliation checks, calculate sale price per residential unit for multifamily properties and retain ordinary sale price for verified one-unit properties.
  4. If unit counts cannot be validated, restrict the principal price comparison to approximately one-unit properties or label the existing result as price per transacted property. Do not call it price per unit.
  5. Report whether 19132 and 19143 remain unusual under each defensible specification.

Also reconcile annual Redfin and RTT price trends. Compare direction, growth, coverage, property definitions, and low-volume years rather than expecting the two sources to produce identical medians.

4.2 Affordability pressure Weeks 4-5

Retain the current price-to-income ratio and add:

  • median gross rent;
  • renter cost burden: share spending at least 30% of income on rent;
  • severe renter cost burden: share spending at least 50%;
  • owner housing costs as a percentage of income;
  • income growth compared with sale-price and rent growth; and
  • an optional mortgage-payment-to-income scenario, clearly labeled as a scenario rather than observed buyer affordability.

4.3 Resident vulnerability Weeks 4-5

Introduce resident vulnerability before the market-pressure results. Compare the two detailed case-study ZIPs with the Philadelphia ZIP/ZCTA distribution and, where geographically comparable, the City estimate on:

  • renter and homeowner share;
  • poverty rate;
  • median household income;
  • educational attainment;
  • senior population;
  • renter and owner housing-cost burden;
  • recent-mover or residential-mobility measures;
  • households receiving public assistance, if retained; and
  • ACS margins of error and data-quality flags.

For neighborhood-composition change, prefer non-overlapping ACS five-year endpoints such as 2010-2014 and 2020-2024. This avoids treating heavily overlapping releases as independent annual observations. Keep the endpoint periods and margins of error visible in the output.

This dimension prevents the analysis from treating appreciation as equivalent to displacement risk. Similar price growth can have different implications in a high-income, owner-occupied ZIP and a lower-income, renter-heavy ZIP.

ACS is a survey. Retain estimates and margins of error together, flag unstable ZIP-level estimates, and state that populations facing housing instability may be difficult to measure fully. Do not describe small percentage differences as substantive when their uncertainty overlaps.

4.4 Observable housing-instability signals Week 5 - priority if available

Prioritize eviction filings as the first outcome-oriented source because both case studies show substantial renter exposure. If usable eviction coverage is not available, document that constraint and select only one alternative:

  • property-tax delinquency or foreclosure;
  • residential turnover;
  • demolition, construction, or conversion permits;
  • investor or corporate purchases; or
  • changes in affordable rental inventory.

Rent burden plus eviction filings, or one documented substitute, would materially strengthen the analysis. Attempting every possible dataset within three weeks would add more integration risk than analytical value.

4.5 Validated trajectory and forecasting analysis Weeks 5-6

Replace unvalidated long-range trend extrapolation with a time-ordered model comparison. Select a target with adequate frequency and coverage, preferably monthly or quarterly transaction activity or price for Philadelphia and the two detailed ZIPs. Compare:

  • seasonal naive or random-walk baseline;
  • ETS;
  • ARIMA; and
  • dynamic regression only when defensible predictors are available.

Use rolling-origin or blocked holdout validation and report MAE, RMSE, and MASE. Pandemic sensitivity is implemented three ways: retain all observations with explicit pandemic and recording-backlog intervention indicators; treat the identified disruption months as missing; and fit only the May 2022 onward regime. All alternatives are judged on the same January 2024-December 2025 holdout. No treatment wins for both ZIPs, so the all-history intervention model is the transparent reference and the alternative paths are retained as a sensitivity range. Display prediction intervals and describe a 2035 path as a long-horizon conditional forecast with substantial uncertainty. XGBoost may be added as a challenger only if the dataset has enough observations and predeclared lagged features; LightGBM is not required.

7. Connecting findings to recommendations Week 6

Recommendations should differ by ZIP and follow directly from the evidence.

Observed condition or scenario Evidence trigger to report Potential City response
High renter burden with rising rent or eviction filings Burden and outcome exceed the Philadelphia comparison Eviction-prevention outreach and rental preservation
Rapid price or per-unit appreciation with vulnerable renters Growth remains unusual after property-type and source checks Preserve naturally occurring affordable housing and consider acquisition support
Increasing pressure on lower-income or senior homeowners Owner burden, delinquency, assessment, or tax evidence supports the concern Promote LOOP, installment plans, tangled-title support, and counseling
Permit or investor activity accelerates in a vulnerable area Activity exceeds the ZIP’s baseline and City trajectory Target preservation, code enforcement, and community acquisition tools
Forecast indicates continued pressure Validated model exceeds its benchmark and intervals remain policy-relevant Begin time-consuming preservation and outreach remedies before the projected peak
Strong growth but lower measured vulnerability Market change is unusual but exposure indicators remain lower Monitor rather than label as a displacement priority
Weak, missing, or uncertain evidence Coverage, MOE, source disagreement, or forecast error is material Improve data collection and continue monitoring

These are policy-alignment recommendations, not claims that one program will causally prevent displacement. Final recommendations should also recognize program eligibility, capacity, budget, and operational constraints.

  10. Evaluate Redfin market context. Add inventory and days on market when definitions and ZIP coverage are adequate. Use these measures as possible explanations for price and transaction patterns, not automatic causes.
  11. Build and validate the forecast. Compare naive, ETS, and ARIMA models with time-ordered validation. Test pandemic sensitivity, report error and intervals, and select a model only if it improves on the baseline.
  12. Apply sensitivity checks. Test alternative base years, minimum sale counts, inflation treatment, property definitions, and exclusion of unusual years. Retain sale-to-assessment only as an appendix sensitivity result.
  13. Draft evidence profiles. Write one evidence statement for each detailed ZIP covering vulnerability, price level and trajectory, transactions, demographic/outcome evidence, forecast uncertainty, and prohibited claims.

9. Required technical cautions Weeks 4-6

  1. [Week 4] ZIP and ZCTA are not identical. Describe the ACS join as a ZIP/ZCTA approximation and document unmatched areas.
  2. [Weeks 4-5] ACS five-year releases overlap. Avoid interpreting every adjacent annual change as a completely new sample.
  3. [Week 4] ACS one-year data are not published for ZCTAs. Use ACS five-year releases for ZIP-level comparisons. If ACS one-year data are used for a Philadelphia citywide benchmark, label the different geography and source separately.
  4. [Weeks 4-5] Margins of error matter. Retain MOE fields and flag unreliable comparisons rather than silently dropping them.
  5. [Week 4] Use constant dollars for long-run growth. Nominal growth can overstate changes in purchasing power.
  6. [Week 4] Redfin observations are rolling periods. Preserve the documented annual aggregation rule and do not describe the source as ordinary monthly sales.
  7. [Weeks 4-6] Sale-to-assessment is an appendix diagnostic. It does not establish mortgage availability, political intent, a tax-bill change, or buyer affordability.
  8. [Week 4] Assessment timing must align with sales. A mismatched assessment year can create an artificial ratio gap.
  9. [Week 5] Medians can hide distributional differences. Report the interquartile range and eligible observation count.
  10. [Weeks 5-6] Correlation is not causation. The analysis identifies conditions that warrant monitoring; it does not prove that price changes caused migration or displacement.
  11. [Week 5] Properties, parcels, and housing units are different. A multifamily property sale is not a single housing-unit sale. Parcel counts do not supply residential-unit counts.
  12. [Week 5] Redfin and RTT measure different samples. Compare their definitions and trajectories; do not expect identical medians or silently choose the source with the stronger result.
  13. [Week 5] Price level, growth speed, and acceleration are different. A below-median ZIP can grow rapidly; CAGR does not measure acceleration.
  14. [Weeks 5-6] ACS is sample-based. Retain MOEs, flag unstable estimates, and acknowledge potential underrepresentation of hard-to-survey residents.
  15. [Weeks 5-6] Forecasts require time-ordered validation. Compare with a naive baseline, test pandemic sensitivity, and display prediction intervals. A long horizon does not become accurate merely because a model produced it.
  16. [Week 6] Two ZIPs are case studies. Findings should not automatically be generalized to every Philadelphia neighborhood.

11. Definition of a successful final deliverable Week 6

The project is complete when the audience can understand:

  1. why the five ZIPs were screened and whether the two detailed selections remain defensible against all qualifying ZIPs and the City benchmark;
  2. how vulnerability differs before market results are introduced;
  3. how price level, growth speed, acceleration, income, and transaction activity differ;
  4. whether Redfin, RTT, and alternative property definitions support the same conclusion;
  5. what the property-versus-unit analysis can and cannot measure;
  6. whether demographic and outcome evidence supports housing-pressure, gentrification, or displacement language;
  7. what the validated forecast adds beyond a simple scenario and how pandemic years affect uncertainty;
  8. which City response is linked to each observed condition or scenario; and
  9. which limitations prevent stronger causal or predictive claims.
